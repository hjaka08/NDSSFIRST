\begin{abstract}
Gigabit Passive Optical Networks (GPON), an ITU-T standardized technology widely adopted in fiber-to-the-home(FTTH) networks, supports downstream payload encryption to protect subscribers' traffic. Unfortunately, encryption does not cover all access-layer metadata. This paper presents a preliminary analysis showing that the Payload Length Indicator (PLI) which remains in plaintext in GEM headers forms a side channel that enables inference of video streaming activity. A passive attacker on the same optical splitter of victim can observe downstream PLI and infer victim's video activity(e.g., Youtube). 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

End-to-end encryption (HTTPS) protects video content, but it does not eliminate side channels. Video identification attacks exploit the structure of HTTP adaptive streaming (HAS): videos are delivered as short segments, and the resulting size/timing bursts depend on the representation (codec/bitrate) and content. Most prior work observes traffic at the IP/Ethernet layer, where segment patterns are blurred by transport and host effects (TCP segmentation, retransmissions, congestion control, and OS scheduling), often requiring longer windows or heavier training.
\textbf{Why GPON may be ``cleaner.''} GPON introduces an underexplored vantage point \emph{below} IP: GEM headers must remain plaintext for real-time multiplexing, and they expose the Payload Length Indicator (PLI). Combined with GPON's fixed 125~$\mu$s downstream framing, this yields a scheduling-aligned, high-rate length signal that is less directly affected by host-side packetization artifacts than IP-layer packet sizes.

\textbf{Takeaways (this poster).} (i) We demonstrate a GPON trace generator that produces access-layer observables (PLI/occupancy) from legitimate encrypted video traffic; (ii) we provide preliminary evidence that PLI-derived signals can detect streaming sessions and indicate ABR changes in a controlled test OLT testbed; (iii) we outline mitigation directions and an evaluation method to quantify privacy--overhead trade-offs.

\textbf{GPON Topology and GTC Framing} GPON Networks consist of an Optical Line Terminal (OLT) at the ISP and multiple Optical Network Terminals/Units(ONTs/ONUs) deployed at subscribers. A single optical tree can serve up to 32 subscribers (e.g., 1:32 split). In the downstream, the OLT transmits downstream traffic through the splitter. Since the splitter passively distributes the optical signal from the OLT to all ONUs/ONTs on the same splitter, each ONU/ONT can physically receive the downstream transmissions destined for other ONUs/ONTs. At the transmission layer, downstream bytes are carried in GPON Transmission Convergence (GTC) frames sent periodically, whose payload area carries multiple GEM frames(described next).

\textbf{GEM Framing} In GPON Network, upper layer traffic (e.g., Ethernet frames carrying TCP/IP packets) in the GEM Payload Field transmitted via GPON Encapsulation Method (GEM) Frames. Downstream GEM payloads are often encrypted for confidentiality (depending on ISP configuration~\cite{itu-g9843}). However, the GEM Port-ID (used to demultiplex services/flows) and the Payload Length Indicator (PLI), which encodes the payload size, are transmitted in plaintext. Thus, an attacker can extract a victim’s encrypted payload lengths from consecutive PLI values (optionally grouped by Port-ID), even without decrypting the payload contents.


\section{Threat Model}
We consider a metadata-only attacker co-located under the same optical splitter as a victim subscriber.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Capability:} passively records downstream GEM headers (no payload decryption; no OLT/operator access).
  \item \textbf{Observable:} a length time series from GEM-PLI (or per-125~$\mu$s frame occupancy) for the victim's Port-ID.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} (1) streaming vs. non-streaming, (2) ABR quality-transition detection, and (3) (future work) content fingerprinting given a reference catalog.
  \item \textbf{Ethics:} all measurements use the author's own traffic on a controlled testbed; we analyze metadata only.
\end{itemize}

From a PCAP of encrypted video traffic, we (i) map Ethernet frames into GEM payloads (fragmenting to satisfy the 12-bit PLI limit), (ii) generate plaintext GEM headers (PLI/Port-ID/PTI/HEC), (iii) encrypt payload bytes with AES-CTR (header remains plaintext), and (iv) pack the GEM stream into fixed-length 125~$\mu$s downstream frames consistent with the configured line rate. The attacker signal is then either the GEM-level PLI sequence or the per-frame occupancy series.

\section{Experiment and Preliminary Results}
We implement a trace-driven, GPON-like downstream simulator to reproduce the access-layer observables exposed by GEM metadata. We capture PCAP traces while streaming YouTube videos under a fixed resolution setting. The simulator fragments Ethernet frames into GEM payloads and synthesizes GEM headers containing Port-ID, PTI, and the 12-bit Payload Length Indicator (PLI). It then packs GEM units into fixed 125,\textmu s downstream time bins, producing a time-aligned sequence of PLI values. Unlike line-rate replay, the simulator preserves original packet timestamps so that idle periods and the bursty structure of adaptive streaming are retained. The simulator outputs structured traces (\texttt{gems.csv}) that expose per-GEM PLI sequences for subsequent analysis. While we do not aim for bit-level equivalence with commercial OLT implementations, our simulator preserves the timing and metadata characteristics relevant to privacy leakage.

\paragraph{Validation protocol.} To validate that these results are not overfitting or dataset leakage, we will (i) use session-level splits (train/test split by video/session, not by window) to prevent overlapping segments across splits, (ii) report confidence intervals via bootstrapping over sessions, (iii) include baselines (random guess, IP-layer-only features, and a simple threshold detector) to contextualize gains, and (iv) perform ablations over background traffic and multiplexing intensity to show whether the signal persists when realistic noise is introduced.

\section{Conclusion And Discussion}
Our results indicate that payload encryption may be insufficient to ensure traffic confidentiality in GPON networks. Although payload contents are encrypted, plaintext fields such as PLI in GEM headers can be used to infer a victim’s video activity(e.g., Youtube). Mitigations may include padding/traffic shaping to dampen length signatures and metadata-aware protocol designs that reduce or obfuscate length exposure at the access layer. Furework includes validating our observations on a real GPON testbed. All code and experimental data is publicly available on the our github repository.~\cite{github}
